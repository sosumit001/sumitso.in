GFF, Mumbai, and the Power of Getting Lost

Some problems, especially in coding, don't get solved by trying harder. The more you stare at the screen, the more tangled the solution seems to get. I'm starting to believe that for problems like these, the only real fix is to walk away—not just to another room, but to a whole other city and a different frame of mind. Well I walked to place I never thought I would be part of it.

The Problem I Left Behind

On a Friday afternoon, my manager told me I needed to go to Mumbai for the Global Fintech Fest. Just like that, I was packing for one of the world's biggest conferences. To be honest, I was relieved to leave my coding problem behind. I figured a few days of not thinking about it couldn't hurt.

I was facing one of those stubborn problems a few weeks ago. I was stuck on my side project, drawbot.chat, a tool that makes diagrams from text, a very simple but really meaningful for me as I write a lot of technical documentation.

New City

Arriving in Mumbai was like diving into an ocean. After nearly a year in the much quieter city of Surat, Mumbai's energy was a total shock. It's a city with its own unstoppable rhythm.

I got my first real taste of it after our first day at the conference. We were exhausted from talking to people and doing demos for hours. When we stepped outside, the traffic was so dense that no taxi would even agree to take us to our hotel. The only option was to walk.

That walk was tough. Our feet, used to office floors, were aching. The air was a mix of humidity, car exhaust, and the smell of street food. We were three tech people, great with software, but we felt completely useless against the simple challenge of walking through a crowded city. It was humbling. And looking back, I think that's when the real problem-solving began. I was forced to unplug from the clean, digital world and deal with messy, real-world challenges.

From Talking to Seeing

For the next three days, my world was our company's booth. It was a cycle of explaining our product at VideoSDK, running a demo, and exchanging business cards. It's a strange kind of performance, and you learn to read people—to see the moment they understand something or the flicker of confusion in their eyes.

The real shift happened on the final day. In the afternoon, the huge crowds started to thin out. The pressure was off, and I finally had a chance to just walk around and see things for myself.

I wandered through the massive halls, no longer as a representative of my company, but just as a curious developer. I saw what dozens of other companies were building. I realized many of them were trying to solve the same kind of problem I was: how do you make something complex feel simple for the user?

I started talking to other developers, and these conversations were different. We weren't selling anything. We were just sharing our struggles. We talked about what worked, what failed, and how they found solutions that seemed so obvious after the fact.

The Realization in the Room

It's a thought that's still becoming clearer, but I don't think my problem with drawbot.chat was ever just about the code. It was about my perspective. Working alone, the problem had started to feel like a giant, impossible mountain.

But listening to others, I saw that everyone deals with similar challenges. My problem wasn't a mountain; it was just a hill. And hearing their stories showed me there were many paths to get to the top.

I left Mumbai with tired feet and a bag full of free stuff. But I brought back something much more important: a new way of seeing my project. The trip had been a reset button for my brain. The chaos of the city, the pain of that long walk, and the ideas I gathered from others all helped me break out of the fixed way I was thinking.

I went to Mumbai to do a job. But the most valuable things happened in the moments that felt the least productive. The solution wasn't found by focusing harder on my screen. It was found by walking away, getting a little lost, and seeing my small problem as a part of a much bigger, shared human experience of trying to build something useful.
